Most bad proposals are not a model problem. They are a brief problem. The draft sounds like a brochure because the prompt asked for a brochure: impressive, comprehensive, professional. Your client asked for a price and a start date.

## A prompt is a brief, not a spell

Treat the model as a fast junior who has never met the client. It will invent confidence if you leave a hole. Your job is to close the holes before it writes: what they said, what you will do, what you will not do, the price you already chose, and one piece of proof you can stand behind.

If you do not know the price yet, do not ask the model to pick one. Ask it to list the missing facts instead.

## The four lines that change the draft

Put these near the top of any prompt that touches a client.

1. Write in my voice: plain, specific, no hype.
2. Use their words for the problem. Here they are.
3. Do not invent testimonials, metrics, or guarantees.
4. Mark every assumption with a check note.

That last line is the one people skip. It is also the one that keeps a wrong date or a guessed budget from looking finished.

## Steal this shape

The proposal prompt on the resource shelf is the full version. The shape is short enough to remember.

- Who they are and where they work.
- The request, pasted, not summarized by you first.
- Three bullets of the work and two bullets of what is out.
- The price and the single next step.
- A word cap, so the draft cannot hide inside length.

Then you edit. The first sentence should be something the client could have said. If it could only have been said by a template, it goes.

## A two-minute check

Read the draft once out loud, or at least with your mouth moving. Cut any sentence you would be embarrassed to say on a call. Check the number, the date, and the names against your notes. Search for words you never use: elevate, seamless, unlock, passionate.

If the proposal is bilingual, do not translate the slogan. Translate the facts. The caption template on the shelf uses the same rule for posts.

## What to stop asking for

Stop asking for ten options. You will pick the longest, not the truest. Stop asking the model to “make it more premium.” Premium, in a service business, is a clear scope and a reply that arrives when you said it would.

Ask for a page a tired person can finish on a phone. Then send it in the hours you already published. The diaspora episode is about that second half: the words can be right and the timing can still wreck the job.
