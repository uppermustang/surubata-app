The AI week is built to take your morning. New model, new feature, a thread that says your current stack is already late. If you run a small business, most of that news is not for you this week. The skill is telling the difference without reading all of it.

## The problem is not the news

A good brief can save you from a tool you do not need, or show you a cheaper way to do a chore you already hate. The damage comes from the third tab, and the fourth, and the feeling that you should have an opinion about all of them before you invoice anyone.

You are not behind. You are unsorted.

## A twelve-minute method

Set a timer. When it ends, you stop, even if a headline is good.

**Minutes 1 to 4. Skim for your job.** Look only for work you already do: follow-ups, proposals, captions, invoices, client questions. If a story is about a lab, a funding round, or a feature you cannot name a chore for, leave it.

**Minutes 5 to 9. Open two.** Not five. Read the summary and the part that says what a person can do tomorrow. If the piece never gets there, close it. Surubata’s briefs are written to end at that point on purpose.

**Minutes 10 to 12. Write one line.** Either “try this on Thursday” with the chore named, or “not for me.” A note that says “interesting” is not a note. Delete it.

## What to ignore on purpose

Ignore benchmarks you will not reproduce. Ignore seat prices for teams of fifty. Ignore any claim that a tool replaces the first human reply. Ignore outrage about a product demo unless the demo changes a price you pay or a chore you do.

Also ignore the urge to switch tools on a Tuesday. A stack earns a change in the monthly review, not in the middle of a headline.

## The only note worth keeping

Keep a single running note with three headings: try, watch, ignore. Try means a chore and a date. Watch means you might care if a price drops or a client asks. Ignore is a parking lot so your brain can release the headline.

Review it on Friday for ten minutes, next to the content checklist. Most “try” lines will look smaller by then. That is the method working.

## When a story deserves more than twelve minutes

Give it a deep read when it changes a cost you pay every month, a rule a client will ask about, or a chore you do twice a week. Then read one long guide, not ten reactions. The weekend stack article is that kind of piece: one Saturday, then you are done thinking about tools until the next review.
